Pitch 3 — Buy the firm that does not need its owner (Family B)

A research memo, not an essay. Written by an agent from public sources on the date stated; every figure is meant to trace to a saved excerpt in the sources/ folder beside this page. Source file in the repository: drafts/pitches/03-owner-independent-acquisition.md.

The unit and the file. One small B2B physical business, bought: a specialist distributor, a B2B textile-care operation or a specialised-cleaning contractor with two to ten staff, asking €280k–400k (succession memo), financed with equity plus Qredits or a BMKB-backed bank loan. The "file" is the deal itself: normalised cash flow, the retained account owner, the working-capital peg, and the debt-service downside written before price is discussed.

The rule and the date. No regulatory date; the clock is demographic. Wholesale has the oldest owners in the CBS series (15.7% aged 65+, 5,210 people) and 94 live listings (boring-business memo); 78% of installation-sector respondents are thinking about sale or succession, half within five years (Techniek Nederland, verified).

Who buys. The reader is the buyer; the seller is a retiring owner whose accountant has one or two listings on Brookz or Bedrijventekoop. Competing buyers are strategic consolidators (in cleaning) and other individuals; the reader competes on process discipline and on paying for diligence the others skip.

Why you. Capital, the engineering habit applied to quoting, job costing and quality, CEE sourcing on the supply side, and crews that are often Polish- or Ukrainian-speaking. The Dutch gate is the customer relationship: the succession memo's rule is that the Dutch account owner stays through the change of control, on paper, before closing.

The precedent. The whole "search fund at small scale" trade; the memos cite Brookz's barometer multiples (3.6× small EBITDA, 4.6–5.7 in cleaning) rather than a single firm. The specialised-cleaning example carries its warning: 16.2% margin, wages at 52.6% of revenue, churn rising (verified, CBS).

The ninety-day test. Broker one paid order between a CEE producer and a Dutch buyer through an existing distributor before bidding on anything: proves the supply edge and puts the reader in front of sellers as an operator, not a tourist. Under €1,000.

Steps two and three. Step two: one acquisition with the seller on an earn-out and the account owner retained. Step three: a second, adjacent firm on the same back office, or the CEE supply line turned into the firm's product.

The AI line. Distribution, textile care and cleaning are handling, logistics and labour; AI takes the back office, which is where the reader's engineering habit already sits.

The kill fact. Normalised cash flow after replacing the seller's unpaid labour cannot service the acquisition debt, or a wage rise under the collective agreement lands after closing. The accountant's normalised figures, checked against payroll, answer it.

The number to settle first. Qredits' and the banks' actual acquisition-finance terms for a first-time buyer with €100k equity (the memo has the ceiling and fee; the conditions are open).

Verdict under the four assumptions. Ranked third. Hands-on, but not beside a job: the day the deal closes the reader runs a company, so this pitch assumes the job ends at step two. Capital is the whole €100k plus debt. If the ceiling is really €50k, this fails; if the reader wants passive income, this fails harder, because the memo's first rule is that owner-independent firms are rare.
