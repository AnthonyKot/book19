Essay 1 of 6 · The cell file · from pitch 2

The integrator signs. You sell the doubt.

A cobot arm arrives with papers. The gripper, fixture, program and worker beside it create a different safety question. A small integrator must answer that question before commissioning the cell. The attractive business is a site visit, measured evidence and a file that makes the answer defensible. The fatal problem is ownership: Dutch integrators already prepare the risk assessment and CE package, and the manufacturer must carry the declaration. This is not a cheap document shop. It is a test of whether an integrator will pay an independent specialist to find the dangerous gap, while keeping the customer and the signature.

The proposition

The first buyer is the owner or engineering lead of a small robot integrator serving Dutch metal, food or packaging plants. The purchase event is commissioning a complete cell when its own risk assessment is nearly done but an awkward application still needs independent challenge. A second event is a tool, payload, speed or guarding change that may invalidate the old reasoning.

The unit is one bounded robot cell. The initial product is a fixed-scope cell-file challenge: review the integrator's risk assessment, inspect the installed cell, witness agreed safety-function tests, execute the agreed force-and-pressure test plan, and issue a signed findings report. The customer receives a technical-file index, evidence gaps and a draft validation record. The integrator closes the gaps, owns the design and signs its declaration.

That boundary matters. The business does not supply the robot, program production motion, design guarding, translate the operating instructions or place its own name on the cell. It does not sell legal approval, certification or a notified-body opinion. It never becomes software with a login. Its tools are calibrated instruments, controlled templates and a defensible record of what was observed.

The substitute is the integrator's own safety engineer, supported by a component vendor and ordinary document templates. Another substitute is a broad machinery-safety bureau that supplies risk assessment, system integration, validation and conformity work as one engagement. Pilz publicly markets that full lifecycle in the Netherlands. The entrant wins only where independence, fast response or Polish-language access to a subcontractor matters enough to buy separately.

The end-user factory is not the first contracting customer. Selling direct would require the entrant to reconstruct an unfamiliar design and negotiate responsibility with the party that built it. The integrator already controls drawings, safety logic, component declarations and commissioning. The narrow offer challenges that work without pretending to own it.

What changed

Start with what did not change. This file is not new. A published Dutch welding-cobot case says the system integrator handled installation, the risk assessment, technical dossier, CE marking and delivery. It also says an external expert reviewed the assessment prepared with Nooteboom's safety specialist. That is buyer behaviour, although the record does not disclose price or who paid the reviewer.

Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 is enacted and applies from 20 January 2027, when it replaces the Machinery Directive. Article 10 requires the manufacturer, before market placement or first service, to prepare technical documentation, conduct the relevant conformity assessment, draw up the EU declaration and affix CE marking. The work therefore remains with the manufacturer. A consultant can prepare and test evidence, but cannot remove that responsibility.

The Regulation does sharpen the retrofit line. Article 18 treats a person making a substantial modification as the manufacturer for the affected machine, or sometimes the affected part of an assembly, according to the risk assessment. That makes a written modification screen saleable: either record why the change is outside Article 18, or scope the new conformity work. It does not mean every new gripper automatically triggers a new CE process.

The revised Product Liability Directive is also enacted at EU level. It treats a substantial modifier outside the original manufacturer's control as a manufacturer and covers products put into service after 8 December 2026. Dutch government guidance describes commencement on 9 December 2026 as expected. An expected national date is not an enacted customer budget, and liability does not prove anyone will buy this service.

Technical practice is moving too. ISO published ISO 10218-2:2025 in February 2025 for industrial robot applications and cells, covering integration, commissioning, operation and maintenance. The accessible EU harmonised-standards record reviewed for this essay did not establish that edition as a cited standard under the new Regulation. Treat the new edition as a technical curriculum, not automatic legal presumption.

Enforcement is real but diffuse. The Dutch Labour Inspectorate says a machinery manufacturer must meet European requirements and affix CE; the employer must keep the machine safe, avoid unsafe changes, maintain it and have it inspected. An incident, insurer question or customer audit can expose a weak file. None creates a ring-fenced purchase order. The commissioning date remains the cleaner sales event.

The number the pitch wanted first cannot be settled from the public record. IFR says it counts industrial installations by country and publishes the data in a purchasable report. TNO's 2026 review says the Netherlands is not explicitly reported in IFR's public 2025 material; it gives only a 2023 density of 264 robots per 10,000 manufacturing workers. TNO also says large-scale cobot use in Dutch SMEs remains limited. Neither source reports annual Dutch cobot installations or the share commissioned without an integrator. Those two figures remain interview questions, not inputs to the economics.

Why you

The engineering habit does the first half of the work. A cell is a system of claims: a hazard maps to a protective measure, the measure maps to a safety function, and the function maps to a test result. The same discipline used to trace a production fault can trace missing evidence. Regulatory literacy helps keep the declaration, risk assessment and technical record separate.

Polish and Russian matter on the supply side, not as decoration. A Polish subcontractor may have designed a fixture, written a PLC block or supplied an incomplete declaration. The reader can resolve that evidence directly. English can work with export-oriented integrators and their engineering managers. The 30,000-person professional audience can surface partners, but attention is not proof of factory trust.

The Dutch gate is not passed by claiming that engineers speak English. Instructions must be in a language users understand; Dutch government guidance says a machine manual must use the language of the country where it is marketed. Shop-floor interviews, residual-risk labels and emergency behaviour can also turn on ordinary Dutch. A2 is not enough to own those conversations.

The answer is a partner and a paper boundary. The Dutch integrator leads operator interviews and supplies the Dutch instructions. The entrant works in English with the engineering lead and in Polish with relevant subcontractors. If the integrator will not own the Dutch-language layer and sign the final declaration, the job fails qualification. The legal sources assign duties to the manufacturer; they do not create a licensed outside CE signatory. An English-language machinery-safety course is commercially available in the Netherlands, but it is training, not statutory authority.

The precedent is Cobots and Machinery Safety Ltd in Corby. Its site sells cobot risk assessments, force-and-pressure testing and CE/UKCA support. Companies House independently records an active engineering consultancy, incorporated on 10 July 2019, with accounts through July 2025 and a June 2026 confirmation statement. That proves a small specialist can persist on the shape. It does not prove Dutch prices or demand.

The constraint that breaks the fit is travel. Measurements happen at a commissioned cell, often during a production stop. If the reader cannot reserve site days alongside the job, regulatory reading has no economic value here. The other break is appetite for liability: a findings report is still professional work around moving machinery.

The first sale

Build ten named prospect types, all narrow enough to have repeat cells: cobot dealers, welding-cell integrators, palletising specialists, machine-tending integrators, packaging-line builders, food-handling automation shops, vision-and-guarding integrators, mobile-robot integrators, retrofit control shops and Polish integrators selling into the Netherlands. Exclude a general factory that has built one experimental cell. It creates no repeat channel.

The opening offer is a two-stage paid diagnostic for one cell already installed but not finally accepted. Stage one is a desk review at a €1,250 design hurdle, credited against stage two. It checks scope, declarations, drawings, risk logic and planned tests. Stage two is the site challenge and evidence pack at a €4,500 total design hurdle. The integrator pays 50% before the desk review and the balance before the site date.

The owner or engineering lead signs because a delayed acceptance, missing test or disputed responsibility can consume senior engineering time. The integrator retains the end-customer contract. It introduces the entrant as independent validation support, not the CE authority. The entrant will not begin without the named manufacturer, cell boundary, intended use, Dutch interview lead and authority to see the relevant design evidence.

Demand outreach should be concrete. Send ten English or Polish notes showing one anonymised evidence map and ask who reviewed the last awkward cell. Ask what the integrator did internally, what an outsider did, what delayed acceptance and what the outsider cost. Do not lead with the 2027 Regulation. The sale is about an installed cell and an acceptance date.

The online record does not settle the pitch's kill fact. IAB publishes €6,000 for CE work on a simple machine and about €30,000 for a machinery assembly, both including a manual. Those are broad anchors, not cobot-cell quotes. The named Dutch integrator and safety-bureau pages reviewed here publish scope but no cell-file price. The record therefore cannot exclude a dealer bundling the work or a competent bureau quoting below €3,000.

Year one is paid learning. Six full jobs require roughly two site days and two desk days each, or twenty-four founder days, plus screens and selling. That can fit around a job only with dates booked weeks ahead. It cannot replace a developer's income, and pretending otherwise would hide the founder's labour.

The mature model barely clears the owner target. It needs about one completed cell each working week at a price above the pitch's original €1,500–€3,000 estimate. The economics therefore settle a second number: a sub-€3,000 complete file is unattractive unless delivery falls below two founder days or is sold as a narrow review. At the full-service scope, price competition can kill the company even when demand exists.

Gross margin pays the delivery engineer, contract review, instruments, language support and travel. Founder replacement is below gross margin, as owner compensation rather than free labour. The €34,000 remainder is company surplus, not additional salary. It must absorb variance, claims, sick time and investment.

Working capital is light only if the boundary holds. Bill 50% in advance, rent the measurement kit until ten paid site jobs, and make the integrator buy any guards, sensors or redesign. The year-one cash-at-risk hurdle is €15,000: up to €4,500 before evidence, then training, insurance advice, standards access, rental deposits and travel from receipts. Do not buy a cell, van or permanent test kit to create demand.

From service to company

Customer one is an integrator with one difficult cell. The founder performs every step. The accumulating asset is a controlled evidence map: intended use, boundaries, hazard, protective measure, safety function, test method, result, deviation, owner and closure. It is a document system, not a customer portal.

Customer five should be at least the second integrator. A contract safety engineer reviews risk logic. A trained technician can prepare instruments, record calibration, execute an approved test plan and assemble the evidence. The founder still qualifies scope, observes unusual hazards and signs the findings report. At least half of routine hours must happen without the founder.

Customer twenty means twenty paying integrators, not twenty cells from one dealer. The twentieth receives the same scope sheet, evidence request, test protocol, exception taxonomy and report structure. Acquisition should arrive through two repeat routes, such as a robot distributor and a safety-component supplier. No channel may control more than a quarter of revenue.

The human work does not disappear. Someone must observe the installed cell, speak with its designer, choose credible contact scenarios and refuse a test that hides the hazard. Delegation belongs in evidence collection, instrument handling, document control and standard cases. The founder bottleneck is judgement at scope changes and the professional signature on findings.

The twentieth delivery differs because prior work has converted ambiguity into qualification rules and reusable protocols. It should take fewer founder hours, not produce a larger template. If every cell remains a bespoke four-day investigation, this is a respectable specialist practice. It is not a scalable third engine.

The incumbent gets a turn

Pilz can sell risk assessment, safety concept, system integration, validation and conformity work in one relationship. IAB's published figures establish a broad CE consultancy price anchor. Integrators can also hire one safety engineer, buy training and keep the learning inside.

The dealer's response is simpler than a price war: bundle the file into the cell, describe outside review as delay, and give the buyer one party to blame. The end customer will often prefer that. A component vendor can loan measurement equipment or include application support to win hardware. A large bureau can cross-subsidise the first cell to sell wider machine-park work.

The partner can capture the margin after learning the protocol. It owns drawings, the end-customer relationship, Dutch shop-floor access and the legal manufacturer role. After three jobs it may train an employee and retain the templates. The entrant's defence is independence across several integrators, calibrated execution, fast scheduling and evidence quality. None is a deep moat.

The modification screen is less exposed to bundling because the original integrator may be gone. It also carries more ambiguity and liability. Keep it as a second product after cell work establishes judgement. Do not use uncertain Article 18 interpretations to frighten workshops into buying.

The capable failure

The company executes well. Its first integrator avoids two commissioning delays, and its reports survive customer review. The partner then standardises three cell designs, buys its own measurement kit and sends an engineer to the same training. Component suppliers improve their application guides. Most new cells become repetitions of a validated pattern.

The remaining outside work is the strange tail: novel tools, uncertain collaboration modes and inherited documentation. Each case needs founder judgement, while ordinary cells stay in-house. The service becomes valuable exactly where it cannot be delegated. Meanwhile the integrator owns the legal signature and tells customers that validation is included.

Nothing failed through poor execution. Successful early work taught the customer to remove the entrant from routine delivery. Revenue becomes irregular expert review, booked around incidents and disputed modifications. That may be excellent paid self-employment. It does not support the mature volume, replacement-cost labour and owner surplus in the table.

The first ninety days

  1. Days 1–15: select ten integrators across the ten prospect types. Run eight English or Polish interviews. Reconstruct the last cell whose acceptance was delayed, who made the file, whether an outsider reviewed it, the paid price and the Dutch-language work.
  2. Days 10–25: ask two machinery-safety specialists to red-team the offer and liability boundary. Take one short English course and buy only the standards needed for the pilot. Cap this combined work at a €2,000 pre-evidence spending hurdle.
  3. Days 20–40: arrange a calibrated measurement-kit rental and professional-indemnity advice. Spend at most a further €1,500 hurdle. Do not buy instruments before a paid site date.
  4. Days 30–60: offer the €1,250 desk diagnostic to five qualified integrators, 50% in advance. Credit it into the €4,500 site challenge. The partner must name the manufacturer, cell, acceptance date and Dutch interview lead.
  5. Days 45–75: deliver one paid challenge beside the day job, with an independent senior reviewer. Time every step. Record missing evidence, corrective work, founder hours, delegated hours and whether the integrator accepts the report boundary.
  6. Days 76–90: offer the same scope to a second integrator and ask the first for a second cell at the mature €5,500 hurdle. Total spend before the first paid diagnostic is capped at €4,500.

Pass: two integrators buy desk diagnostics, one converts to a paid site challenge, and one offers a second cell at the mature price. The partner owns Dutch instructions and the final declaration. Delivery shows a route to fewer than two founder days per repeat cell. Kill: five qualified offers produce no paid diagnostic; any credible provider quotes a complete comparable cell file below €3,000; all five integrators bundle validation internally; or every buyer requires the entrant to lead Dutch operator interviews. The national installation count is not a pass criterion. Payment through two channels is better evidence.

Verdict

TEST THROUGH A PARTNER

The integrator supplies the legal manufacturer, cell evidence, Dutch shop-floor layer and repeat channel. The single assumption that would most change the verdict is that two independent integrators will pay the mature €5,500 hurdle for an outside challenge while retaining the signature. If direct factories buy that scope in English and supply Dutch instructions themselves, the partner may be unnecessary. If integrators bundle it below €3,000, do not enter.
